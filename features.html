One engine, three ways to ask

The three methods share a design description, a thermal load, and a result format — so you can move between them without re-entering anything. They are demonstrated here on two different packages, and the distinction matters when you read the numbers: the resolution evidence below comes from a study of an AMD MI350-class package, while the speed evidence comes from a 2nm CoWoS design whose absolute microns are indicative rather than settled. Each section says which is which.

Fidelity

High-fidelity at various resolution

Customers usually ask for a mesh: 50 µm is the common number. But resolution is something you choose — it is an input, not a result, and no single run can tell you whether the one you picked was fine enough. The question worth asking is the one you can actually check: does the answer still change when you refine? On the package below, it does not — which means a resolution you can afford already carries the answer a far finer one would give.

What this buys you

Explore coarse, commit fine. Because the answer has stopped moving well above the resolution you were told to ask for, early design work can run at a resolution that costs minutes on a laptop instead of a day on a machine you would have to buy. You spend the fine-resolution budget once, late, on the localized region that actually needs it — rather than paying it on every candidate in a sweep.

Global exploration

WarpStack-GNN — sweep the whole design space

A network trained on WarpStack's own 3D output returns the full warpage map without solving. It is global: once trained for a design, it answers anywhere in that design's material and thermal space, in milliseconds. The figures below are measured on the 2nm CoWoS reference design.

Training data comes from WarpStack’s own 3D formulation, generated far more efficiently than a brute-force sweep of solves — 1,792 labelled warpage fields in 32 minutes rather than the day a design point at a time would cost. Past 120 design points the network is cheaper than solving directly, and a 1,000-point sweep drops from 11.5 hours to 83 minutes.
What to know before you rely on it. A network is trained per design: changing a thickness or a footprint changes the model and requires retraining, and the checkpoint refuses to load against a design it was not built for rather than returning plausible numbers for the wrong stack. Accuracy is reported against WarpStack's own 3D method at a fixed resolution, not against measurement. Generalization to designs outside the one a network was trained on has not yet been measured.
Localized exploration

WarpStack-MOR — localized design-space exploration

Localized means something precise here: the compact model is built over a bounded box around a nominal design — by default ±10% on each material property — and inside that box every answer carries a rigorous error bound with it. Ask for a point outside the box and it still answers, still reports its bound, and tells you that you have left the region it was built for. Change the floorplan and it refuses to load at all.

A what-if the model was never built at

ABF substrate stiffened 24.0 → 28.8 GPa
798,000× faster

The WarpStack-MOR is a physics-based compact model built once per design from the same 3D formulation — not a black-box fit to data. Here the substrate is stiffened, a change that moves peak-to-peak warpage by +40.9 µm. The WarpStack-MOR resolves that shift to within 0.036 µm — in 51 µs rather than 40.6 s.

Both maps are the active surface at the same mesh and the same thermal load, so this compares the two methods like for like rather than against measurement. On this package the active and continuous package surfaces agree to within 0.1% of peak-to-peak — the bow is global, so the die-level and package-level views coincide.
